# Style and anti-slop pass

Use this reference for substantial prose that has become generic, inflated,
repetitive, or conspicuously model-written. Apply it after the information
architecture is sound. Sentence polish cannot repair a response organized
around the writer's process.

## Remove generic framing

Delete openings that merely announce complexity, importance, nuance, or the
act of answering. Begin with the answer, issue, or concrete situation.

Avoid stock phrases that could prefix almost any response. Do not tell the
reader that a point is “worth noting,” “important to understand,” or “a key
consideration”; state the point and let its consequence establish importance.

## Prefer real relationships to rhetorical emphasis

Name cause, contrast, condition, consequence, or uncertainty directly. Do not
use dashes, sentence fragments, bold labels, or repeated “not X but Y”
constructions as substitutes for reasoning.

Use emphasis sparingly. A page where every paragraph contains bold text has no
meaningful emphasis.

## Keep language concrete

Replace vague abstractions with the actor, action, object, and consequence when
those are known. Prefer “the retry can create two charges” to “there are
potential idempotency implications.”

Do not turn verbs into abstract nouns without a reason. Prefer “decide,”
“measure,” and “fail” to “make a decision,” “perform measurement,” and
“experience a failure.”

Keep one stable term for one concept. Do not cycle through synonyms to create
variety when the result makes the reader wonder whether the subject changed.

## Control rhythm without performing style

Vary sentence and paragraph length when the reasoning benefits. Break a long
sentence that carries several independent claims. Join choppy fragments when
their relationship matters.

Use lists only for parallel items. Use paragraphs when sequence, causality, or
qualification is the point. Avoid a stack of one-sentence sections that turns a
continuous argument into interface chrome.

## Remove model-shaped residue

Delete:

- restatements of the prompt;
- conclusions that repeat the opening without adding a consequence;
- fake quotations or invented reader reactions;
- overbroad lessons extracted from one example;
- canned transitions and symmetrical three-part lists created for cadence;
- promises that something is “clear,” “simple,” or “straightforward” instead
  of making it so;
- self-congratulation, process narration, and claims about thoroughness.

Do not mechanically ban a word or punctuation mark. Remove a pattern when it
weakens meaning, not merely because models often use it.

## Read aloud for friction

On the final pass, look for:

- a sentence whose subject arrives too late;
- a pronoun with more than one plausible antecedent;
- a qualification separated from its claim;
- a term that changes meaning;
- a paragraph that contains more than one controlling idea;
- a heading that says less than the paragraph below it.

Make the smallest edit that removes the friction. Preserve the writer's useful
voice rather than replacing it with generic polish.
