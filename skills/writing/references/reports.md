# Reports and analyses

Use this guidance for substantive reports meant to be read directly by a user:
research syntheses, analyses, audits, investigations, reviews, and decision
briefs. A report is an interface to evidence, not a transcript of the work that
produced it.

## Find the governing question

Complete this sentence before choosing sections:

> As a **[reader]**, I need to understand or decide **[question]**, so that I can
> **[outcome]**.

Ask only when materially different answers would produce materially different
reports. Do not organize around research chronology, source order, subagents,
tools, or implementation components.

## Build an answer hierarchy

Use Situation-Complication-Question-Answer to discover the real question, then
use the Pyramid Principle to organize one governing answer supported by a small
number of distinct, collectively sufficient points. Each supporting point
should lead to the evidence and reasoning that earn it.

Treat these as thinking tools, not visible template headings. The governing
answer may be a qualified conclusion, a map of unresolved tensions, or a
finding that the evidence is insufficient. Answer-first writing makes
uncertainty legible; it does not suppress it.

Order information from highest to lowest consequence:

1. What the reader must know or decide.
2. What supports, explains, qualifies, or challenges it.
3. Background, method, provenance, and reference detail.

Apply this order to the report, sections, and paragraphs.

## Make the first layer independently useful

A reader who sees only the opening and scans the headings should recover the
governing answer, the support structure, the material limits, and the route to
deeper evidence.

Use headings that carry conclusions or name a specific subject. “Two lifecycle
gaps cause the failures” is more useful than “Findings.” A heading hierarchy
should expose the report's argument when read by itself.

Place methodology, implementation detail, SQL, logs, long quotations, source
inventories, and exhaustive taxonomies in subordinate sections, appendices, or
linked evidence artifacts unless they change the interpretation, confidence,
or decision. Progressive disclosure relocates useful detail; it does not erase
it.

## Keep epistemic roles visible

Distinguish:

- **Evidence:** what sources or observations establish.
- **Interpretation:** what follows from that evidence and why.
- **Recommendation:** what the writer thinks should be done.
- **Decision:** what an authorized person has chosen.

Do not let one role impersonate another. Put limitations and meaningful
counterevidence beside the claims they qualify, not in a distant disclaimer.

An analytical paragraph often moves through claim, evidence, reasoning or
implication, and a material qualification. Use that as a reasoning pattern, not
as four labels every paragraph must display.

## Separate colliding artifacts

If one document is simultaneously an argument, evidence archive, methodology
paper, reference catalog, and operational runbook, separate those functions
into layers or companion artifacts. Keep a dense evidence pack intact when it
still serves audit or agent continuation, and link it from the reader-facing
report.

Do not prepend an executive summary to an unchanged dump when the body still
forces the reader to reconstruct the argument.

## Test the reader's route

Scan only the title, opening, headings, and closing synthesis. Confirm that the
reader can explain the answer, supporting logic, material limits, and where to
inspect the evidence.

Then read as someone arriving cold, short on time, skeptical of unsupported
claims, and uninterested in the production process. Repair the hierarchy where
that reader would lose the thread or mistake inference for fact.
